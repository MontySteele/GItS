### Arm 1 — Second Helping

1. **Card involvement.** The prototype was played first by both graders; Opus seriously weighed its ordering because “any attack ahead of Second Helping risks draining the bank,” while Codex considered no second line.

2. **Change in kind of choice.** Yes: the shipped half merely discharged Spark automatically, whereas the prototype created a choice about whether Second Helping or another Attack received the bank.

3. **Did the cost bind?** Yes. Spending Spark on Second Helping removed the automatic discount from later Attacks; the replay then charged their real energy costs and refused Rapid Fire, also preventing Duck and Cover.

4. **Text problem?** Neither grader misread Second Helping’s printed effect; the replay contradicts their claim that every displayed-0 Attack remained free, which is the disclosed shared display/rule problem.

5. **Judgment: ADVANCE.** One surviving grader articulated a genuinely steered Spark destination and its opportunity cost; the other’s `no_second_line` refusal weakens replication but does not contradict that evidence.

### Arm 2 — Rummage

1. **Card involvement.** Both graders played Rummage first; Opus weighed skipping it to preserve Spark, and Codex “seriously considered attacking before Rummage.”

2. **Change in kind of choice.** Yes, though less dramatically: Eager to Help only rewarded preserving the bank until it was read, while Rummage explicitly competed with the automatic Attack discount for ownership of it.

3. **Did the cost bind?** Yes. Rummage spent Spark and 1 Energy, leaving Rapid Fire at its real unaffordable cost; the replay refused it before either Attack damage or Duck and Cover could occur.

4. **Text problem?** Neither grader misread Rummage’s face, but both forms’ affordability assumptions are contradicted by replay because the displayed Attack costs ceased to apply after Spark was spent.

5. **Judgment: ADVANCE.** Both graders independently recognized the bank-allocation branch, including the concrete trade between three cards now and an Attack discount now or later.

### Arm 3 — Slow Burn

1. **Card involvement.** Both graders led with Slow Burn; Opus weighed attacking first or preserving Spark, while Codex considered attacks first but rejected that because they would consume the Sparks Slow Burn needed.

2. **Change in kind of choice.** Yes: Imaginary Friend leaves Spark inside the Attack economy, whereas Slow Burn lets the player divert it into Block and Burst progression.

3. **Did the cost bind?** Yes. After Slow Burn spent Spark and 1 Energy, Rapid Fire was immediately unaffordable in one replay; in the other ordering only Kaboom! remained affordable, and Duck and Cover was also lost.

4. **Text problem?** Opus misread the Burst accounting: it predicted two separate +5 additions, but Slow Burn moved Burst only from 20 to 35, while Imaginary Friend moved it from 20 to 28. Codex did not state a contradictory meter total.

5. **Judgment: RETURN.** Return the board and presentation for rework: the Spark diversion is real, but the displayed-cost failure makes the apparent “play everything” line fictitious, and the Burst wording produced a replay-disproved reading.

Overall, the round supports the narrow claim that explicit Spark spenders can turn an automatic bank into a player-steered allocation, most cleanly in Arms 1 and 2. Nothing warrants **ESCALATE**: disagreements about Duck and Cover’s intent sensitivity are peripheral and resolvable, while the graders agree on the prototype-relevant ordering. The display defect does not erase the qualitative ordering evidence, but it prevents conclusions about actual line closeness, affordability, or the magnitude of each prototype’s opportunity cost.
