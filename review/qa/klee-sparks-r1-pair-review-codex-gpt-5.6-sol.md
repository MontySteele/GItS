Q1. The price as a decision

The readers consistently saw that the bank could not cover every priced card, but three of the constrained boards mostly resolved to “take the larger immediate result.”

- t02: all readers treated Fwoosh! as dominating Ka-pow!, not as a genuine hold/spend decision. The local tester called Ka-pow! merely “the 7 damage…because only one Spark card could be paid for.” The same-family reader was explicit: “the only real choice between them is which prints the larger number; Fwoosh! does,” and called Ka-pow! “strictly one damage worse.”

- t03: this produced the clearest actual spend-versus-hold comparison. The local tester called the alternative “close because it preserves a Spark at the cost of only 2 damage this turn.” The same-family reader likewise compared “banking 1 Spark for a later turn at the price of 2 damage now.” Both ultimately took the larger immediate result, but both visibly recognized the banked Spark as the thing surrendered.

- t06: both readers saw the same choice, though the one-enemy staging flattened both AoE cards into ordinary single-target damage. The local tester compared 14 damage with spending both Sparks against 11 damage while leaving one; the same-family reader explicitly “weighed banking that Spark against the 3 extra damage now.” Both took the larger result.

- t07 is not actually a board where the bank could not pay for both sinks. It began at 4, exactly enough for Firework Finale and Fwoosh!, and Powder Pop increased it further. The same-family reader correctly said, “the hand is entirely affordable, so the only real decision was sequencing.” Its serious alternative was instead “holding Firework Finale” because it Exhausts. The local tester simply pursued “the maximum printed damage available.”

Thus the printed prices made resource exclusivity and the forgone bank visible on t03 and t06, but did not by themselves overcome immediate-number dominance. The separate fight simulation is consistent with this: the arm spends less than the retired automatic rule, while not simply accumulating unusable Spark indefinitely. That supports asking the question in whole fights, not treating these isolated selections as its answer.

Q2. The price on the page

No reader misstated a printed Spark price, miscounted the available Spark bank, or treated an unaffordable priced Attack as free.

The forms repeatedly read the text correctly:

- t01: “with the Spark bank empty, Ka-pow! is printed as unplayable,” followed by Powder Pop to unlock it.
- t02: “Fwoosh! and Ka-pow! cost 0 energy but compete for the same one Spark.”
- t03: both readers distinguished spending one Spark from spending two and correctly calculated the resulting bank.
- t04: the same-family reader correctly said that with exactly 3 Spark, Oath permitted “exactly one Attack.”
- t05: readers correctly understood that the 1-Spark Ka-pow! was affordable while Oath-converted Kaboom! cards would require Spark the bank did not contain.
- t06 and t07: all Spark deductions were stated correctly.
- t08: both readers understood that the two priced cards were initially locked and that Powder Pop enabled only one of them.

There were other mechanical errors, but not price-reading errors. The tester’s t05 claim that Duck and Cover was effectively unavailable/use-less with 1 Energy remaining was the caught energy-cost misread; it was not a Spark-price misread.

Q3. The Rare Power

Yes, readers distinguished the two bank states from the page.

At t04, with 3 Spark, the same-family reader understood that Oath enabled exactly one conversion: “with exactly 3 Spark I can pay for exactly one Attack.” It treated Oath as potentially meaningful setup but not as the payoff on this particular turn, saying a defensive or buffing intent “would have made Oath tempting.” The local tester did not engage with Oath at all, although its chosen ordinary-Energy line was mechanically valid.

At t05, with only 1 Spark, the same-family reader correctly described the immediate result as nonfunctional for the converted Kaboom! cards: after Ka-pow!, “both Kaboom!s would want 3 Spark each and I would have 0 Spark.” It still identified Oath as a possible long-game setup, but called the observable turn vastly inferior. My earlier blind read found no serious second line at all.

So the page communicated “one conversion available” versus “conversion unavailable.” Oath read as conditional setup/payoff at t04 and as an immediate brick at t05; it was not mistaken for an unconditional discount.

Q4. The empty bank

On both boards, the priced card read as dead before generation and as a plan once Powder Pop was noticed.

At t01, the same-family reader said Ka-pow! was “printed as unplayable” and that “Powder Pop is what unlocks it.” My earlier read used exactly that sequence. At t08, the same-family reader again said “both Spark spenders are locked out until something banks one,” then selected one after Powder Pop generated the Spark.

No reader tried to play a priced card directly from the empty bank. The blocked message therefore communicated a present dead card, while the generator made it a visible sequencing plan within the same hand.

Q5. The tester seat

The local model is not yet reliable enough to hold the tester seat as the deciding reader.

It usually named a mechanically meaningful second line:

- t03 compared spending both Sparks with preserving one.
- t04 compared additional damage against enough Block to cover the intent.
- t05 compared damage against Block.
- t06 compared present damage against retaining Spark.
- t08 compared the two competing 1-Spark attacks.

Its weak point was intent engagement. Five of its eight forms answered “No” to whether another intent would change the line: t01, t02, t03, t06 and t08. Some of those boards were genuinely intent-insensitive—the same-family reader also failed t03 and t08—but t02 and t06 show the tester playing Duck and Cover against a Debuff while saying it had “no downside.” The other reader correctly recognized that Block “does nothing this turn” and preserved the card.

More seriously, two of the tester’s eight chosen lines could not be replayed. At t01 and t07 it wrote Powder Pop without a target, and both replays stopped immediately with: “Card requires a target. Provide ‘target’ with an entity_id.” Those forms are untested rather than disproved, but a quarter of the seat’s lines failed the basic replayability requirement. The same-family lines on both boards replayed cleanly.

The caught t05 misread adds another reliability concern: the tester treated Duck and Cover incorrectly despite the page printing its energy cost. Its final line then claimed that no remaining card could use the unspent Energy, although Duck and Cover was available.

Overall tester-seat judgment: RETURN. It can articulate alternatives, but its intent reasoning and replay-valid line construction are not dependable enough for this seat.
